## 1. Interruption science

Scope note: numbers below are quoted from the primary PDFs where they could be read (marked **[primary read]**); figures only remembered from secondary summaries are marked **unverified**.

### 1.1 Fragmented knowledge work (Mark, González & Harris 2005, CHI) — [primary read]
Source: Mark, G., González, V. M., Harris, J. "No Task Left Behind? Examining the Nature of Fragmented Work", CHI 2005. PDF: https://ics.uci.edu/~gmark/CHI2005.pdf (ACM: https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1054972.1055017).
Observational study of 24 information workers (analysts, developers, managers), ~700 h of shadowing.
- Average time in a "working sphere" before switching or being interrupted: **11 min 4 s** (sd 18 min 9 s).
- **57% of working spheres were interrupted**; interrupted spheres lasted longer (12 min 40 s) than uninterrupted ones (8 min 58 s).
- **77% of interrupted work was resumed the same day**, after an average of **2.3 intervening working spheres**; the authors note the user must reconstruct not only the task state but a changed environment (documents opened/closed by the intervening spheres).
- Self-resumed work was resumed after **21 min 28 s** on average (sd 46 min 47 s); externally-resumed work after **61 min 37 s** (sd 95 min 11 s), t(987)=7.05, p<.001. 90.1% of resumptions were self-initiated.
- Collocated workers worked longer per sphere (11 min 56 s vs 9 min 56 s) but were interrupted more; distributed workers resumed more (82.1% vs 74.3% same-day).
- Design implication stated by the authors: "external technological reminders could prove useful when people are interrupted after working only brief periods", and the amount of state to preserve grows with the number of intervening tasks.
- The widely quoted "**23 minutes 15 seconds** to return to a task" is a figure Mark gave in interviews about this line of work; it does not appear in the 2005 PDF (the paper's own figure is 21 min 28 s for self-resumed work) — treat 23:15 as **unverified in the primary**.

### 1.2 Interrupted work is faster but more stressful (Mark, Gudith & Klocke 2008, CHI) — [primary read]
Source: "The Cost of Interrupted Work: More Speed and Stress", CHI 2008. PDF: https://ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf (ACM: https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1357054.1357072).
Lab experiment (48 participants, email-answering task; baseline vs same-context vs different-context interruptions by phone/IM).
- Interrupted participants **completed the task in less time with no difference in quality**; the authors interpret this as a compensation strategy (working faster, writing less).
- Price: significantly higher **NASA-TLX workload** (F(2,92)=3.82, p<.03), **stress** (F(2,92)=12.15, p<.001), **frustration**, **time pressure** (F(2,92)=4.71, p<.01) and **effort** in both interruption conditions vs baseline (20-point TLX scales; e.g. stress 10.83–11.50 vs lower baseline).
- **Interruption context did not matter**: same-topic interruptions were not cheaper than different-topic ones ("any discontinuity in the task creates a disruption cost").
- Personality (openness to experience, need for personal structure) predicted disruption cost.

### 1.3 Resumption lag and the memory-for-goals model (Altmann & Trafton; Trafton et al.; Monk et al.)
- **Altmann & Trafton 2002**, "Memory for goals: an activation-based model", *Cognitive Science* 26(1):39–83, https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog2601_2 — suspended goals decay; resumption needs retrieval cues; priming during the *interruption lag* (the seconds between alert and interruption) reduces the *resumption lag*. (Model paper; cited via the papers below — **not read directly**.)
- **Trafton, Altmann, Brock & Mintz 2003**, "Preparing to resume an interrupted task: effects of prospective goal encoding and retrospective rehearsal", *IJHCS* 58:583–603, https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Trafton-IJHCS03.pdf — [primary read]. 17 NRL employees; **Warning** condition got an **8 s interruption lag** (alert + frozen input), **Immediate** got none. Warning participants prepared far more (95% of preparation utterances fell in the interruption lag; Immediate participants prepared only 3.1 times in 30 opportunities, χ²(1)=53.5, p<.001) and **resumed faster**; Immediate participants caught up with practice.
- **Altmann & Trafton 2004**, "Task Interruption: Resumption Lag and the Role of Cues", *CogSci 2004*, https://interruptions.net/literature/Altmann-CogSci04.pdf — [primary read]. **Resumption lag 3.8 s vs 1.9 s inter-action interval** in the same task ("double"); cues available immediately *before* the interruption reduce the lag afterwards (cue-availability effect); experiments used interruption lags of 2, 4, 6 and 8 s.
- **Altmann & Trafton 2007**, "Timecourse of recovery from task interruption: Data and a model", *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 14(6):1079–1084, https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03193094 — resumption cost decays over the first few actions after resuming (first post-interruption action slowest). **Not read directly** (abstract-level, from memory): unverified detail.
- **Monk, Trafton & Boehm-Davis 2008**, "The effect of interruption duration and demand on resuming suspended goals", *JEP: Applied* 14(4):299–313, https://doi.org/10.1037/a0014402 — abstract read via search snippet (ResearchGate/Trafton site): "**longer and more demanding interruptions led to longer resumption times**"; the resumption-time profile for durations up to 1 min "supported the role of decay", and the duration×demand interaction "the importance of goal rehearsal in mitigating decay". Exact resumption-time values **not read** (PDF fetch failed): unverified.
- **Altmann, Trafton & Hambrick 2014**, "Momentary Interruptions Can Derail the Train of Thought", *JEP: General* 143(1):215–226, https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030986 (PDF https://interruptions.net/literature/Altmann-JExpPsycholGen14.pdf) — [primary abstract read]. Interruptions averaging **4.4 s tripled** the rate of sequence (place-keeping) errors; interruptions averaging **2.8 s doubled** it; non-sequence errors unaffected; latencies showed smaller effects than errors. Implication: even a 3-second glance away is costly for procedural, step-keeping work (exactly what an engineer in an editor/terminal is doing).

### 1.4 Deferring to breakpoints (Bailey, Iqbal, Adamczyk)
- **Adamczyk & Bailey 2004**, "If not now, when? The effects of interruption at different moments within task execution", CHI 2004, https://doi.org/10.1145/985692.985727 — interrupting at predicted "best" moments (coarse task boundaries) vs "worst" moments: best moments produced less annoyance, frustration and time pressure and better recall; **unverified** (could not read the PDF; cited from memory and the Wikipedia "Interruption science" summary https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interruption_science).
- **Bailey & Konstan 2006**, "On the need for attention-aware systems: Measuring effects of interruption on task performance, error rate, and affective state", *Computers in Human Behavior* 22(4):685–708, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2005.12.009 — peripheral tasks interrupting primary tasks mid-task increased primary task completion time (commonly summarised as **+3% to +27%**), **doubled error rate**, and raised annoyance (+31% to +106%) and anxiety (about twice); effect smaller at task boundaries. Direction confirmed by Wikipedia "Attention management" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attention_management: "higher completion time, double task error rate as well as increase the annoyance"); the exact percentages are **unverified** (paywalled).
- **Iqbal & Bailey 2005–2008** (breakpoint detection, OASIS). "Effects of Intelligent Notification Management on Users and Their Tasks", CHI 2008, https://interruptions.net/literature/Iqbal-CHI08.pdf — [primary read]. OASIS defers notifications to detected task **breakpoints** (Coarse / Medium / Fine, from task hierarchy). Field-style study: "scheduling notifications at breakpoints **reduces users' frustration and reaction time** relative to immediate" delivery, "balanced against a **deferral time of 1.5 minutes on average**"; relevance of notification content determined which breakpoint type users preferred. Prior Iqbal & Bailey work cited therein shows "coarser breakpoints correspond to lower cost" (resumption lag, frustration, errors). Earlier: Iqbal & Bailey 2005 "Investigating the effectiveness of mental workload as a predictor of opportune moments for interruption" (CHI EA 2005) and 2006 "Leveraging characteristics of task structure to predict the cost of interruption" (CHI 2006, https://doi.org/10.1145/1124772.1124882) — titles from memory, **unverified** details.

### 1.5 Attention-sensitive notification (Horvitz et al., Microsoft Research)
- **Horvitz, Jacobs & Hovel 1999**, "Attention-Sensitive Alerting", UAI 1999 (the *Priorities* system): decision-theoretic alerting that weighs the expected cost of interruption against the expected cost of delayed review of a message, inferring attention from desktop activity; **not read directly** (unverified summary; original at https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/2073796.2073832).
- **Horvitz 1999**, "Principles of Mixed-Initiative User Interfaces", CHI 1999, https://doi.org/10.1145/302979.303030 (*LookOut* calendaring agent: acting under uncertainty, timing of service, dialog vs action thresholds) — **not read directly**; unverified summary.
- **Horvitz & Apacible 2003**, "Learning and Reasoning About Interruption", ICMI 2003, https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/learning-and-reasoning-about-interruption/ — [abstract read]: methods to infer "the cost of interrupting users based on multiple streams of events including information generated by interactions with computing devices, visual and acoustical analyses, and data drawn from online calendars"; the *Interruption Workbench* captured events and trained models of expected interruption cost.
- **Horvitz, Koch & Apacible 2004**, "BusyBody: Creating and fielding personalized models of the cost of interruption", CSCW 2004, https://doi.org/10.1145/1031607.1031690 — intermittently asks users their interruptibility to train per-user models; **not read directly** (Microsoft page 404), unverified.
- **Horvitz, Apacible & Subramani 2005**, "Balancing Awareness and Interruption: Investigation of Notification Deferral Policies" (bounded deferral), User Modeling 2005; and Horvitz, Achlioptas & al., "Principles of Bounded Deferral for Balancing Information Awareness with Interruption", https://erichorvitz.com/bounded_deferral.pdf — [primary read, abstract]: "notification policies that defer incoming alerts for a bounded time if a user is busy when alerts arrive", with a decision-theoretic formulation of the maximal deferral.
- **Iqbal & Horvitz 2007**, "Disruption and Recovery of Computing Tasks: Field Study, Analysis, and Directions", CHI 2007, https://interruptions.net/literature/Iqbal-CHI07.pdf — [primary read]. 27 users, two weeks of logging: primary tasks were interrupted by **3.74 alerts/hour** on average (4.28 email + 3.21 IM alerts/h in the respective sub-populations); maximum time on one application before a switch was just over 4 minutes, average under one minute. **40.8%** of email alerts got an immediate response (<15 s), leaving on average **3 task windows suspended**; the other 59.2% were answered after **7 min 32 s** on average (self-initiated switch). Users tabbed through ~7.5 applications to reach the alerting app. The paper's resumption figures (time to return to the suspended task) lie past the portion read: the commonly quoted "~10 minutes on the alerting app plus further time in peripheral apps, and 27% of suspensions taking > 2 h to resume" is **unverified** here.

### 1.6 Negotiated vs immediate vs mediated vs scheduled (McFarlane 2002) — [primary read, abstract and summary]
Source: McFarlane, D. C. "Comparison of Four Primary Methods for Coordinating the Interruption of People in Human-Computer Interaction", *Human-Computer Interaction* 17(1):63–139, 2002, https://www.interruptions.net/literature/McFarlane-HCI02_2.pdf (DOI https://doi.org/10.1207/S15327051HCI1701_2).
- Taxonomy of coordination: **immediate** (interrupt now), **negotiated** (announce, let the user choose when), **mediated** (an agent decides based on the user's state), **scheduled** (deliver at fixed times).
- Experiment with 36 participants in an abstracted multitasking game: "**Negotiation support is the best overall solution except where small differences in the timeliness of handling interruptions is critical and then immediate is best.**" Companion review: McFarlane & Latorella 2002, "The Scope and Importance of Human Interruption in HCI Design", same issue.
- Detailed per-measure results (accuracy, completeness, promptness trade-offs) sit deeper in the 77-page article and were not read; the headline is from the abstract.

### 1.7 Self-interruption (Dabbish, Mark & González 2011)
Source: "Why do I keep interrupting myself?: Environment, habit and self-interruption", CHI 2011, https://doi.org/10.1145/1978942.1979405. From memory / secondary summaries — **unverified** figures: in the field data roughly **half of interruptions were self-initiated** (Mark 2005 reports internal vs external interruptions as roughly comparable in count); self-interruption rose after hours in which people had been externally interrupted (external interruptions appear to "prime" later self-interruption), and was higher in open-plan offices. Implication kept to the sourced direction only: a supervisor UI that pulls the user back into agent chatter trains self-interruption habits.

## 2. Supervisory control and automation

Legend: **[read]** = source text read during this recon; **[not read]** = cited from memory of the primary, summary unverified against it today.

### 2.1 Levels of automation — Sheridan & Verplank 1978; Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens 2000
- Sheridan, T. B. & Verplank, W. L. (1978) *Human and Computer Control of Undersea Teleoperators*, MIT Man-Machine Systems Lab technical report — the original 10-level scale from "computer offers no assistance; human does it all" (1) through "computer suggests one alternative" (4), "executes that suggestion if the human approves" (5), "allows the human a restricted time to veto before automatic execution" (6), "executes automatically, then necessarily informs the human" (7), "informs the human only if asked" (8), "informs only if it, the computer, decides to" (9), to "decides everything, acts autonomously, ignoring the human" (10). **[not read]** — level wording reproduced from memory of the widely reprinted table; unverified.
- Parasuraman, R., Sheridan, T. B. & Wickens, C. D. (2000) "A Model for Types and Levels of Human Interaction with Automation", *IEEE Trans. SMC-A* 30(3):286–297, https://doi.org/10.1109/3468.844354 — automation can be applied at four information-processing **stages** (information acquisition, information analysis, decision selection, action implementation), each at a **level** on the 10-point scale; the level should be set by evaluating primary criteria (mental workload, situation awareness, complacency, skill degradation) and secondary criteria (automation reliability, costs of decision/action outcomes). **[not read]** — unverified summary.
- Translation to an agent inbox: an agent's "ask" is a request to operate at level 4–5 (suggest + wait for approval) at the *decision-selection* stage; a "finished work" item is level 7 (act, then necessarily inform) at the *action* stage. Level 6 (veto window) is the model for "Esc-undo within 2 s". Which stage an item sits at should be visible, because the costs of a wrong answer differ by stage (Parasuraman et al. 2000 argue action-implementation automation needs the most caution).

### 2.2 Situation awareness and the out-of-the-loop problem — Endsley
- Endsley, M. R. (1995) "Toward a Theory of Situation Awareness in Dynamic Systems", *Human Factors* 37(1):32–64, https://doi.org/10.1518/001872095779049543. Three levels, confirmed in Wikipedia's summary **[read]** (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Situation_awareness): **Level 1 perception** ("perceive the status, attributes, and dynamics of relevant elements"), **Level 2 comprehension** ("synthesis of disjointed Level 1 SA elements through the processes of pattern recognition, interpretation, and evaluation"), **Level 3 projection** of near-future states.
- Out-of-the-loop performance problem: the same page **[read]** states "Automation is a major factor reducing situation awareness in many environments (e.g. aviation, driving, power operations) … people are forced to become monitors which they are poor at (due to vigilance problems)". Primary: Endsley, M. R. & Kiris, E. O. (1995) "The Out-of-the-Loop Performance Problem and Level of Control in Automation", *Human Factors* 37(2):381–394, https://doi.org/10.1518/001872095779064555 — **[not read]**; from memory: intermediate levels of automation preserved SA better than full automation.
- Translation: an agent rail that only shows a colour per agent supports Level 1 at best; comprehension (why is it blocked, what did it just change) and projection (what will it do next, how long until it needs me) require one more glance-level of information per agent, or the user is a monitor rather than a supervisor.

### 2.3 Ironies of Automation — Bainbridge 1983
- Bainbridge, L. (1983) "Ironies of Automation", *Automatica* 19(6):775–779, https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8. Wikipedia summary **[read]** (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ironies_of_Automation): automating most of the work leaves the operator "responsible for tasks that can not be automated", so "operators will not practice skills as part of their ongoing work", their work "now also includes exhausting monitoring tasks", and "rather than needing less training, operators need to be trained more to be ready for the rare but crucial interventions"; ~1 800 citations by Nov 2016 (Strauch).
- Translation: the supervisor of coding agents is handed exactly the residue Bainbridge describes — monitoring plus the hardest judgement calls — with less hands-on context than if they had written the code. The inbox must therefore carry the context the agent had (diff, test output, criteria) rather than assume the supervisor has it.

### 2.4 Trust in automation — Lee & See 2004; complacency and automation bias
- Lee, J. D. & See, K. A. (2004) "Trust in Automation: Designing for Appropriate Reliance", *Human Factors* 46(1):50–80, https://doi.org/10.1518/hfes.46.1.50_30392 — **[not read]**; from memory: the goal is *calibrated* trust (trust matching capability), *resolution* (trust distinguishing good from bad situations) and *specificity*; design should make the automation's purpose, process and performance visible; over-trust yields misuse (complacency), under-trust yields disuse.
- Parasuraman, R. & Manzey, D. H. (2010) "Complacency and Bias in Human Use of Automation: An Attentional Integration", *Human Factors* 52(3):381–410, https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720810376055 — **[not read]**; complacency and automation bias share an attentional root and grow with workload and multitasking. Wikipedia "Automation bias" **[read]** (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automation_bias): "more automation can decrease cognitive workload but increase the opportunity for monitoring errors".
- Translation: an inbox that is answered by keystroke in < 1 s invites automation bias (approve-by-reflex). Mitigations with research backing: show the agent's evidence (process), show a per-agent reliability history (performance), and make the "approve" default no faster than the "look" action.

### 2.5 Management by exception
- In supervisory-control terms (Sheridan's levels 7–9), the human is informed only of deviations; the operator sets the thresholds. The classic formal treatment is Sheridan, T. B. (1992) *Telerobotics, Automation, and Human Supervisory Control*, MIT Press — **[not read]**. Industrial alarm standards (2.7) are the operationalised form: an alarm exists only if it requires an operator response.
- Translation: every inbox item must have an action the user and only the user can take; status that needs no response is not an inbox item (it belongs on the rail or in a digest).

### 2.6 One operator, many vehicles — fan-out, neglect time, interaction time
- Olsen, D. R. & Goodrich, M. A. (2003) "Metrics for Evaluating Human-Robot Interactions", PerMIS 2003 — defines **Neglect Time (NT)**: how long a robot can be ignored before its performance drops below a threshold; **Interaction Time (IT)**: time to bring it back to peak; **Fan-Out (FO) ≈ NT / IT**, i.e. the number of robots one operator can supervise (later refined as FO = (NT + IT)/IT by Olsen & Wood 2004, "Fan-out: measuring human control of multiple robots", CHI 2004, https://doi.org/10.1145/985692.985722). **[not read]** today (PDF fetch 404); formulas from memory, unverified.
- Crandall, J. W., Goodrich, M. A., Olsen, D. R. & Nielsen, C. W. (2005) "Validating Human-Robot Interaction Schemes in Multitasking Environments", *IEEE Trans. SMC-A* 35(4):438–449 — neglect-tolerance model; Crandall, J. W. & Cummings, M. L. (2007) "Developing performance metrics for the supervisory control of multiple robots", HRI 2007, https://doi.org/10.1145/1228716.1228722 — adds **wait times** (time a vehicle waits for operator attention: queue wait, interaction wait, situation-awareness wait) as the cost that grows with fan-out. **[not read]**; unverified.
- Cummings, M. L. & Mitchell, P. J. (2008) "Predicting Controller Capacity in Supervisory Control of Multiple UAVs", *IEEE Trans. SMC-A* 38(2):451–460, https://doi.org/10.1109/TSMCA.2007.914757 — discrete-event simulation of operator utilisation; from memory **[not read]**: performance degrades sharply when operator **utilisation exceeds ~70%**, and wait times caused by the operator's queue dominated other losses; Cummings & Guerlain (2007, *Human Factors* 49(1)) found operators could handle up to ~12 Tomahawk missiles in a simulation with a flattening of performance beyond ~8 — unverified figures.
- Translation to agents: NT is how long an agent runs usefully without the supervisor (minutes to hours for a coding agent that has a clear brief and gates); IT is the time to read its ask and answer. Fan-out rises by lengthening NT (better briefs, self-verification, done_when) and shortening IT (packet with evidence inline, 1/2/3 keys) — both are UI/brief levers, and the queue wait time of agents blocked on the human is the metric to watch (Crandall & Cummings).

### 2.7 Alarm flooding and alarm-management benchmarks — EEMUA 191 / ISA-18.2
- EEMUA Publication 191 *Alarm Systems — A Guide to Design, Management and Procurement* (3rd ed. 2013, ISBN 978-0-85931-192-2; publisher page https://www.eemua.org/Products/Publications/Print/EEMUA-Publication-191.aspx **[read, catalogue only]**) and ANSI/ISA-18.2-2009 (now ISA-18.2-2016) *Management of Alarm Systems for the Process Industries*, with IEC 62682 as the international twin — both confirmed as the governing standards by Wikipedia "Alarm management" **[read]** (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alarm_management); the Wikipedia article also records that EEMUA 191 was written with ASM Consortium field data.
- Benchmark figures (from memory of EEMUA 191 §6 / ISA-18.2 Annex and the many vendor summaries; **unverified today** because the standards are paywalled and web search was degraded): steady-state **"very likely to be acceptable" ≈ 1 alarm per 10 min (≈ 6/h, ≈ 150/day)**; **"manageable" ≈ 2 per 10 min (≈ 300/day)**; above that "likely to be over-demanding"; after a plant upset the operator should receive **≤ 10 alarms in the first 10 minutes**; an *alarm flood* is defined as **> 10 alarms per 10 minutes**, and the system should be in flood **< 1% of the time**; priority distribution target ≈ **80% low / 15% medium / 5% high** (some texts 80/15/5 with ≤ 1% critical); stale alarms (standing > 24 h) fewer than ~5 at any time. ISA-18.2 adds a lifecycle (philosophy → rationalisation → design → implementation → operation → maintenance → monitoring/assessment → management of change) and the principle that **every alarm must require an operator response** ("an alarm is an audible and/or visible means of indicating … a malfunction, process deviation, or abnormal condition requiring a timely response").
- Translation: an agent inbox is an alarm system with one operator. Its design budget is **~6 items/hour steady, ≤ 10 in any 10-minute burst**, a flood counter, rationalisation (no item without a required human response), and a priority mix where almost nothing is "high".

## 3. Attention, memory and perception

### 3.1 Working-memory limits — Miller 1956 vs Cowan 2001
- Miller, G. A. (1956) "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two", *Psychological Review* 63(2):81–97, https://doi.org/10.1037/h0043158 — span of absolute judgement and immediate memory ≈ 7 ± 2 chunks; Miller himself treated the coincidence of the two numbers as suggestive, not a law. **[not read today]**.
- Cowan, N. (2001) "The magical number 4 in short-term memory: A reconsideration of mental storage capacity", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 24(1):87–114, https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922 — when rehearsal and chunking are prevented, capacity of the focus of attention is **about 4 chunks (3–5)** in young adults. Wikipedia "Working memory" **[read]** (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Working_memory): "Cowan proposed that working memory has a capacity of about four chunks in young adults (and fewer in children and old adults)"; "The focus is regarded as having a limited capacity and holds up to four of the activated representations"; Oberauer adds a one-chunk inner focus.
- Translation: a supervisor can hold roughly four agents' *situations* in mind at once; beyond that the rail must externalise state (persistent glyph + one-line reason) rather than rely on memory. Each inbox item should fit in one chunk: one question, one default.

### 3.2 Hick–Hyman law and Fitts's law
- Hick, W. E. (1952) "On the rate of gain of information", *Quarterly J. Exp. Psychology* 4(1):11–26, https://doi.org/10.1080/17470215208416600; Hyman, R. (1953) "Stimulus information as a determinant of reaction time", *J. Exp. Psychology* 45(3):188–196. Wikipedia "Hick's law" **[read]** (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hick%27s_law): decision time rises **logarithmically** with the number of equally probable choices, RT = a + b·log₂(n + 1) ("the +1 takes into account the uncertainty about whether to respond"); "twice as many choices does not result in twice as long to choose". Unequal probabilities (a strongly expected default) lower the effective information and the time.
- Fitts, P. M. (1954) "The information capacity of the human motor system in controlling the amplitude of movement", *J. Exp. Psychology* 47(6):381–391, https://doi.org/10.1037/h0055392 — movement time grows with log₂(2D/W); targets at screen edges and under the cursor are fastest. **[not read today]**.
- Translation: a 3-option question with a recommended default costs ~log₂(4) = 2 bits before the default's prior is counted; with a strong default the decision approaches 1 bit. Keyboard answers (1/2/3) remove the Fitts cost entirely; an edge rail (infinite-width target along the screen edge) is the Fitts-optimal placement for a pointer-driven fallback.

### 3.3 Preattentive features and distinguishable categories — Healey & Enns; Ware
- Healey, C. G. & Enns, J. T. (2012) "Attention and Visual Memory in Visualization and Computer Graphics", *IEEE TVCG* 18(7):1170–1188, https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2011.127 (and Healey's long-running survey page https://www.csc2.ncsu.edu/faculty/healey/PP/) — a limited set of features (hue, luminance, orientation, size, motion/flicker, curvature, 2-D position) is detected **preattentively**, in ≈ 200–250 ms regardless of distractor count; conjunctions of two features are not. **[not read today]**; unverified timing.
- Ware, C. (2012) *Information Visualization: Perception for Design*, 3rd ed., Morgan Kaufmann — recommends at most **~8–10 distinguishable colour categories** for labelling (fewer under small size/peripheral viewing), ~12 well-chosen if large; shapes similar; the number of preattentively separable *states* per glyph is small (he suggests ≤ 4–5 levels on a single channel for reliable reading). **[not read today]**; thresholds unverified.
- Translation: each rail glyph should encode **one preattentive channel per meaning** (e.g. hue = needs-you / working / done / failed; motion only for "needs you now"), with **≤ 4 states** per channel, no conjunction codes ("blue *and* pulsing means…").

### 3.4 Peripheral and ambient displays
- Matthews, T., Dey, A. K., Mankoff, J., Carter, S. & Rattenbury, T. (2004) "A toolkit for managing user attention in peripheral displays", UIST 2004, https://doi.org/10.1145/1029632.1029676 — the **Peripheral Display Toolkit**: abstraction (summarise), **notification levels** (ignore / change blind / make aware / interrupt / demand attention) and transitions mapped to perceptual salience. **[not read today]**; level names from memory.
- Pousman, Z. & Stasko, J. (2006) "A taxonomy of ambient information systems: four patterns of design", AVI 2006, https://doi.org/10.1145/1133265.1133277 — four dimensions: **information capacity** (1 to > 20 sources), **notification level** (ignore → demand attention), **representational fidelity**, **aesthetic emphasis**; four patterns: symbolic sculptural display, multiple-information consolidators, information monitor display, high-throughput textual display. **[not read today]**.
- Weiser, M. & Brown, J. S. (1996) "The Coming Age of Calm Technology" (Xerox PARC; later in *Beyond Calculation*, 1997) — calm technology "informs but doesn't demand our focus or attention"; it moves easily between the **periphery and the centre of attention** and increases our use of the periphery. Wikipedia "Calm technology" **[read]** (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calm_technology) lists the three principles: attention resides mainly in the periphery; the technology increases use of the periphery; it gives *locatedness* (placing the user in the context).
- Case, A. (2015) *Calm Technology: Principles and Patterns for Non-Intrusive Design*, O'Reilly; principles at https://calmtech.com/ — "technology should require the smallest possible amount of attention", "should inform and create calm", "should make use of the periphery", "amplify the best of technology and the best of humanity", "can communicate, but doesn't need to speak", "should work even when it fails", "the right amount of technology is the minimum needed to solve the problem". **[not read today]**; wording from memory.
- Translation: the edge rail is a peripheral display at Pousman & Stasko's *information monitor* pattern; its default notification level should be *change blind / make aware*, with *interrupt* reserved for needs-you items and *demand attention* for nothing short of a failing destructive action.

### 3.5 Change blindness and a live rail
- Rensink, R. A., O'Regan, J. K. & Clark, J. J. (1997) "To See or Not to See: The Need for Attention to Perceive Changes in Scenes", *Psychological Science* 8(5):368–373, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1997.tb00427.x; Simons, D. J. & Levin, D. T. (1997) "Change blindness", *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 1(7):261–267 — changes that coincide with a visual disruption (blink, saccade, flicker, a window redraw) are routinely missed unless attention is on the changed item. **[not read today]**.
- Translation: a rail that updates silently will have its state changes **missed**; an item that changed state should carry a short transient marker (a persistent "new since you looked" dot, not a one-off flash that can be missed during a saccade), and the rail should animate changes over ~200–300 ms rather than swap instantly. Conversely, continuous motion defeats the periphery (Weiser & Brown; Matthews et al.) — motion must stop when no new information exists.

## 4. Cognitive Load Theory

- Sweller, J. (1988) "Cognitive Load During Problem Solving: Effects on Learning", *Cognitive Science* 12(2):257–285, https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4; Sweller, J., van Merriënboer, J. J. G. & Paas, F. (1998) "Cognitive Architecture and Instructional Design", *Educational Psychology Review* 10:251–296, https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1022193728205; updated in Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas (2019) "Cognitive Architecture and Instructional Design: 20 Years Later", *Educ. Psychol. Rev.* 31:261–292, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5 (which notes the field's move to treat germane load as the share of working memory devoted to intrinsic load rather than a third additive load). **[not read today]**.
- Three loads (Wikipedia "Cognitive load" **[read]**, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_load): **intrinsic** — "the inherent level of difficulty associated with a specific instructional topic" (element interactivity); **extraneous** — "generated by the manner in which information is presented"; **germane** — "the working memory resources that the learner dedicates to managing the intrinsic cognitive load … if the intrinsic load is high and the extraneous load is low, the germane load will be high".
- **Split-attention effect** (Chandler & Sweller 1992, "The split-attention effect as a factor in the design of instruction", *British J. Educ. Psychology* 62:233–246, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8279.1992.tb01017.x; Ayres & Sweller 2005 in the *Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning*): when mutually referring sources (a diagram and its text, a diff and the test that failed) are separated in space or time, the reader must hold one while searching for the other; physically integrating them lowers extraneous load and improves performance. **[not read today]**.
- **Redundancy effect** (Chandler & Sweller 1991, *Cognition and Instruction* 8(4):293–332; Kalyuga, Chandler & Sweller 1999): presenting the same information twice in different forms (a self-explanatory diagram plus text repeating it; a summary plus the same facts again) **adds** load for competent readers rather than helping — the **expertise reversal effect** (Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller 2003, *Educational Psychologist* 38(1):23–31, https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4) says material that helps novices hurts experts. **[not read today]**.
- **Worked-example and completion effects** (Sweller & Cooper 1985; Paas 1992): a presented solution to study costs less than deriving one; relevant to "show me what you did and why" over "here is the raw log, decide".
- **Measurement**: Paas, F. (1992) 9-point subjective mental-effort scale (*J. Educational Psychology* 84(4):429–434); NASA-TLX (Hart & Staveland 1988, see §7); Leppink et al. (2013) 10-item instrument separating intrinsic, extraneous and germane load (*Behavior Research Methods* 45:1058–1072, https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-013-0334-1). **[not read today]**.

### 4.1 Application to a review/decision screen
| CLT effect | Design consequence for an inbox / review packet |
|---|---|
| Intrinsic load is fixed by the decision; extraneous load is the UI's fault | Reduce element interactivity per screen: one decision, its options, its evidence; defer everything else behind "look at" |
| Split attention | Evidence **inline with the question** (diff hunk beside the claim, test output beside the criterion); never "see thread" / "open the log" as the primary path; time-split is as costly as space-split, so no modal-then-context sequences |
| Redundancy / expertise reversal | For a daily user: no boilerplate preamble, no restated context the user already has; a terse packet with expandable detail (progressive disclosure) rather than a long narrative; novices (first week) get the longer form |
| Worked example | Agent states its recommendation and the reason in one line; the user verifies rather than derives |
| Germane load | What is left should be the real judgement (is this the right behaviour for users?), which is the point of keeping a human in the loop at all |
| Measurement | A one-tap Paas 9-point effort rating after a sample of inbox answers is the cheapest CLT instrument (see §7) |

### 5.1 Developer-productivity evidence
- **METR 2025 RCT** — "Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity", https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/ **[read]**. 16 experienced open-source developers, 246 real issues on their own repositories, randomised AI-allowed vs AI-disallowed (Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet mostly). Result: "when developers use AI tools, they take **19% longer** than without"; developers "expected AI to speed them up by **24%**, and even after experiencing the slowdown, they still believed AI had sped them up by **20%**"; developers were paid $150/h. The perception–reality gap is the point for a supervisor UI: self-report of time saved is unreliable, so §7 needs logged measures.
- **Peng, Kalliamvakou, Cihon & Demirer 2023**, "The Impact of AI on Developer Productivity: Evidence from GitHub Copilot", arXiv:2302.06590 — 95 developers, HTTP-server task, Copilot group **55.8% faster** (n.s. on completion rate). **[not read today]**; figure from memory.
- **Cui et al. 2024 / Microsoft–Accenture–Fortune 100 field experiments** ("The Effects of Generative AI on High Skilled Work", SSRN 4945566) — ~26% more completed pull requests with Copilot across ~4 800 developers; **unverified** (not read).

### 5.2 Levels of autonomy for agents
- **Feng, McDonald & Zhang 2025**, "Levels of Autonomy for AI Agents", arXiv:2506.12469, https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.12469 **[abstract read]**: five levels "characterized by the roles a user can take when interacting with an agent: **operator, collaborator, consultant, approver, and observer**"; autonomy is "a deliberate design decision, separate from its capability and operational environment"; proposes "AI autonomy certificates" for single- and multi-agent systems.
- **Shavit, Agarwal, Brundage et al. - **Anthropic, "Building effective agents" (Dec 2024)**, https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-effective-agents **[read]**: "the most successful implementations use simple, composable patterns rather than complex frameworks"; recommends "finding the simplest solution possible, and only increasing complexity when needed"; distinguishes *workflows* (predefined code paths) from *agents* (LLM-directed); the autonomous-agent section (from memory of the same page, **unverified wording**) says agents should "pause for human feedback at checkpoints or when encountering blockers" and have stopping conditions. Companion engineering posts "How we built our multi-agent research system" (June 2025) and "Effective context engineering for AI agents" (Sept 2025) — **not read today**.
- Mapping to design 130: the Inbox is the *approver* role (Feng level 4); the Router is *operator/collaborator*; the rail is *observer*. A design that lets the user move an agent between these roles per task is what both the Feng and OpenAI papers call for.

## 6. Design principles derived

Each principle names the finding it rests on (section reference) and the evidence grade: **A** = primary read today, **B** = primary known but not read today, **C** = secondary/unverified.

1. **One needs-you queue, and nothing in it without a required human action.** — Management by exception and the alarm-rationalisation rule "an alarm requires a response" (§2.5, §2.7, grade B/C); Mark 2005: 2.3 intervening activities per interruption, so each extra place to look multiplies resumption cost (§1.1, A).
2. **Interrupt only at a breakpoint — the agent's (end of a step, a gate) and the user's (idle, app switch, after a save).** — Iqbal & Bailey 2008: deferral to breakpoints cut frustration and reaction time at a 1.5-min average delay (§1.4, A); Adamczyk & Bailey 2004 (C); Horvitz bounded deferral (§1.5, A-abstract): defer while busy, up to a bound.
3. **Negotiate, don't seize: announce an item and let the user pull it; take the immediate path only when timeliness is critical.** — McFarlane 2002: negotiated best overall, immediate only when small timing differences matter (§1.6, A-abstract); Trafton 2003: an 8-s interruption lag lets people encode a resumption goal (§1.3, A) — so the rail glyph change *is* the warning and the inbox opens only on the user's keystroke.
4. **Rate-cap and batch: ≈ 1 item per 10 min steady, ≤ 10 per 10 min burst; beyond that, collapse into a digest ("flood mode").** — EEMUA 191 / ISA-18.2 benchmarks (§2.7, C); Iqbal & Horvitz 2007 field baseline of 3.74 alerts/h already being disruptive (§1.5, A).
5. **≤ 4 states per glyph, one preattentive channel per meaning, no conjunction codes, motion only for "needs you now".** — Cowan's ~4 chunks (§3.1, A via Wikipedia summary); Healey & Enns / Ware preattentive limits (§3.3, B); Weiser & Brown periphery (§3.4, A-summary).
6. **≤ 3 options per question with a recommended default; keys 1/2/3.** — Hick–Hyman: log₂(n+1) with a strong prior shrinking the effective choice (§3.2, A-summary); Fitts: keys beat pointer travel (§3.2, B); Cowan: one chunk per item (§3.1).
7. **Evidence inline with the question; never make the user re-derive what the agent already knows.** — CLT split-attention and worked-example effects (§4, B); Bainbridge: the supervisor has less context than the actor (§2.3, A-summary); Endsley L2/L3: comprehension and projection need the "why" and "what next" (§2.2, A-summary).
8. **Show the cost of answering (estimated time to resolve, and whether the agent is blocked on it) so the user can choose when to pay it.** — Fan-out economics: interaction time and queue wait time govern how many agents one person can run (§2.6, B); Horvitz: expected cost of interruption vs cost of delay (§1.5, A-abstract); Mark 2008: people compensate for interruptions by rushing, at a stress cost — a visible "this can wait" removes the pressure to rush (§1.2, A).
9. **Receipts and undo (Esc within 2 s) so fast answers are cheap to correct.** — Sheridan level 6 (veto window) as a safer substitute for level 7 (§2.1, B); overreliance research: speed and reflex approval rise together, so make the fast path reversible (§5.3, B/C).
10. **Keep the user in the loop with periodic low-cost summaries, not streams.** — Endsley out-of-the-loop problem and vigilance (§2.2, A-summary); Pousman & Stasko: an *information monitor* at the "make aware" notification level (§3.4, B); Mark 2005 self-resumption 21 min vs 61 min external — a summary that lets the user self-resume beats a push (§1.1, A).
11. **Protect ongoing work from micro-interruptions: no toasts over the active window while the user is typing; even 2.8 s away doubles place-keeping errors.** — Altmann, Trafton & Hambrick 2014 (§1.3, A-abstract); Altmann & Trafton 2004 resumption lag 3.8 s vs 1.9 s (A).
12. **Allow management by exception per agent: the user sets which classes of asks auto-resolve (Feng's "observer" role) and which require them (the "approver" role), with reliability history visible.** — Sheridan levels 7–9 and Parasuraman 2000 stage/level model (§2.1, B); Lee & See calibrated trust via visible purpose/process/performance (§2.4, B); Feng et al. 2025 roles (§5.2, A-abstract).
13. **Add a forcing function for high-stakes items (show the hunk before accepting, second key for destructive actions).** — Buçinca et al. 2021 cognitive forcing functions (§5.3, B); automation-bias literature (§2.4).
14. **Make state changes noticeable but not animated continuously: a persistent "changed since you looked" marker, ~250 ms transitions, then stillness.** — Change blindness (§3.5, B); calm technology (§3.4, A-summary).
15. **Instrument the surface rather than ask users whether it saved time.** — METR 2025: a 19% slowdown perceived as a 20% speed-up (§5.1, A).

## 7. Metrics to evaluate the design

Grounding: Mark 2005/2008 and Iqbal & Horvitz 2007 are field-measurable analogues (§1); NASA-TLX (Hart & Staveland 1988, "Development of NASA-TLX (Task Load Index): Results of Empirical and Theoretical Research", in *Human Mental Workload*, North-Holland, pp. 139–183, https://doi.org/10.1016/S0166-4115(08)62386-9 — six 20-point scales: mental, physical, temporal demand, performance, effort, frustration; Mark 2008 used a modified TLX with a stress scale added **[read in Mark 2008]**); Paas 1992 single-item effort scale; Olsen & Goodrich neglect/interaction time; ISA-18.2 KPIs (alarm rate, flood %, stale count, priority mix).

| Metric | Definition in theuth terms | Instrument | Target / reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Interruptions per hour** | Items that moved from rail to foreground (toast, panel open, sound) per active hour | event log on the companion layer | ≤ 6/h steady (ISA/EEMUA analogue); field baseline 3.74 alerts/h was already disruptive (Iqbal & Horvitz 2007) |
| **Burst count** | Max items surfaced in any rolling 10 min | same | ≤ 10; % of time in "flood" < 1% |
| **Time-to-answer (TTA)** | Item created → answered, split by priority and whether the agent was blocked | engine timestamps (attention items table) | report median and p90; watch p90 for blocked items |
| **Agent wait-for-human time** | Sum of time agents sit blocked on a human answer, per agent-hour (Crandall & Cummings wait time) | engine | trend down as fan-out rises |
| **Fan-out proxy** | Concurrent agents ÷ (user interaction minutes / agent-hours) ≈ NT/IT | derived | rising fan-out at flat TLX = success |
| **Inbox dwell time** | Panel open → closed, per item answered | UI | should be seconds for 1/2/3 items; long dwell = evidence missing (split attention) |
| **Resumption-lag proxy** | Time from inbox close to first keystroke/click in the previously focused app (macOS frontmost-app + input-event timestamps; no content capture) | companion layer, opt-in | compare with Altmann & Trafton's 3.8 s vs 1.9 s scale; track trend not absolute |
| **Working-sphere length** | Time in the frontmost non-theuth app between theuth foregroundings | same | Mark 2005 baseline 11 min 4 s; should not fall after adopting the companion layer |
| **Undo rate** | Esc-undo within the window ÷ answers; also later reversals (task moved back, approval revoked) | engine | low but non-zero (zero means the window is unused or too short); spikes = reflex approvals |
| **Reflex-approval rate** | Answers given < 1 s after the item appeared, for items whose packet had unopened evidence | UI | monitor; forcing function for high-stakes items |
| **Deferral latency cost** | Delay added by breakpoint deferral (item ready → surfaced) | event log | Iqbal & Bailey saw 1.5 min average as acceptable |
| **Subjective load** | NASA-TLX (6 scales, or RTLX unweighted) at end of day during A/B weeks; Paas 9-point effort after a 1-in-20 sample of answers | in-app prompt, ≤ 10 s | lower TLX at equal or higher throughput |
| **Perceived vs logged time saved** | Weekly one-question estimate vs logged metrics | in-app | expect a gap (METR 2025); report both |
| **Priority mix** | Share of items marked high/medium/low | engine | ≈ 5/15/80 (alarm-management analogue); if "high" > 20%, priorities are not rationalised |
| **Stale items** | Items older than 24 h still open | engine | < 5 at any time (alarm analogue) |

Method notes: run the companion layer in A/B weeks against the current Attention inbox (design 104) with the same agents; log everything locally (sessions.sqlite) and never capture screen content for the resumption proxy — only app identity and input-event timing; a two-week window matches the Iqbal & Horvitz 2007 study length.

## Implications for theuth (short)

The research supports the One-style layer as a *peripheral display plus a rationalised queue*: a rail at Pousman & Stasko's "make aware" level with ≤ 4 glyph states, an inbox that is rate-capped and breakpoint-deferred (McFarlane's negotiated mode by default, immediate only for blocked-and-urgent), items that carry the agent's evidence inline (CLT split-attention), ≤ 3 options with a default and keyboard answers (Hick, Fitts), a 2-s undo as Sheridan's veto level, and per-agent autonomy settings (Feng roles) with visible reliability. The single most important discipline is that of alarm management: nothing enters the queue unless it needs the human, and the queue's rate is measured. Instrument TTA, agent wait time, interruptions/hour and undo rate from day one; do not rely on perceived time saved.
